**From Revision to Independent Argument: Conditions for Learning from Formative Feedback**

Formative feedback can improve a draft without necessarily improving the writer’s capacity to construct a later argument independently. L5 distinguishes correcting a text from learning to select relevant evidence and judge the conclusions it supports when a particular comment is absent. A warrant is the reasoning connecting evidence to a conclusion, whether or not it appears as an explicitly labelled sentence. [L5, p. 2, ¶1] This distinction makes performance on a new task central to the present question: successful revision alone cannot establish independent learning.

The strongest aggregate evidence concerns argument quality, usually assessed through claim clarity, evidence relevance and treatment of counterarguments. Across 18 randomized or quasi-experimental comparisons involving 2,140 novice university students, L1 reported a pooled standardized mean difference of 0.31 (95% CI 0.16–0.46) favouring feedback over usual instruction or no additional feedback. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 61%), and eight comparisons raised concerns about allocation or assessor masking. Fourteen comparisons assessed revisions, with an estimate of 0.38 (95% CI 0.22–0.54); only four assessed new tasks two to six weeks later, yielding 0.12 (95% CI −0.08–0.32). These results provide clearer evidence for revision gains than for later performance, but without a reported interaction estimate they do not establish that the subgroup effects differ statistically. The new-task interval also leaves both benefit and harm possible. [L1, pp. 4–5, ¶1] Because L1 does not specify whether the individual studies below contributed to its synthesis, they cannot be treated as wholly independent confirmation. [L1, p. 6, ¶2]

L2 provides more specific evidence that a structured revision activity can support later writing. In a first-year course, 120 students were individually randomized to usual instructor comments or the same comments plus a worksheet asking them to identify which claim each cited finding supported and justify their revisions. Evidence materials, writing time and the single revision opportunity were held constant, and assessors were masked. The worksheet group scored 0.6 points higher on the revised essay (95% CI 0.2–1.0) and 0.5 points higher on an independent essay with a new evidence packet three weeks later (95% CI 0.1–0.9), both on a 0–10 argument-quality scale. The later task prohibited worksheet access, supporting an effect extending beyond assisted revision. However, follow-up analysis included only 108 students, with six missing per group; equal missing counts do not establish that attrition was inconsequential. [L2, pp. 7–8, ¶1]

That extension was not observed for every feedback intervention. L3 randomized 90 novices to immediate automated suggestions about clarity and evidence placement or ordinary written rubric guidance, with one 45-minute revision session per group. All students completed both assessments, which were scored by masked assessors. The tool group gained 0.9 more points on the revised draft (95% CI 0.4–1.4), but the difference on a new essay without the tool two weeks later was 0.0 points (95% CI −0.4–0.4), on a 0–10 scale. This interval does not establish equivalence. [L3, pp. 3–4, ¶1] L2 and L3 thus illustrate why revision and independent performance require separate assessment. They do not establish that explanation worksheets outperform automated feedback: their comparators, follow-up periods and rubrics differed, with L2 assessing engagement with conflicting evidence and L3 emphasizing coherence and evidence placement. [L2, p. 8, ¶1; L3, p. 4, ¶1]

The worksheet findings are consistent with L5’s proposal that explaining revisions could provide practice in judging evidential support, but consistency does not demonstrate the proposed process. L5 also warns that explanations may simply repeat an instructor’s wording. [L5, p. 3, ¶2] In L2, 11 of 16 interviewed worksheet users said the worksheet made them notice that a relevant citation might not justify a claim’s strength. These retrospective accounts came from volunteers; the other five accounts and recruitment procedures were not described. They therefore cannot establish changed understanding or mediation. Moreover, the intervention combined explanation prompts with a written revision record, leaving their contributions unresolved. [L2, p. 8, ¶¶1–2]

Observational evidence supports examining explanation while underscoring the difficulty of identifying its contribution. Among 180 first-year writing-clinic volunteers in L4, students who explained feedback use in a revision memo scored 0.7 points higher on a subsequent 0–10 argument task (95% CI 0.2–1.2), adjusting for baseline writing and prior course grade. Because explanation was not randomized, unmeasured motivation or instructor contact could account for the association; follow-up missingness was also unreported. [L4, p. 10, ¶1] This finding complements L2 by identifying a similar pattern in a voluntary clinic, but does not independently establish that explanation causes improvement.

The proposal in L5 also depends on students being able to interpret feedback and having an opportunity to act on it. [L5, p. 3, ¶2] In L4, students with lower baseline scores more often described phrases such as “develop the warrant” as difficult to apply, suggesting an accessibility problem without demonstrating an advantage of simpler wording. [L4, p. 11, ¶2] The evidence also cannot identify a preferred comment format. L1 distinguished directive comments proposing specific changes from questions asking writers to justify choices, but found frequent combinations of both and too few adequately described comparisons to estimate a reliable difference. Delivery, practice time and revision opportunities also varied. [L1, p. 6, ¶2]

Together, these sources provide a conditional answer: adding structured explanation and a revision record to usual comments improved independent writing in L2, but the evidence does not establish explanation alone as necessary or sufficient for improvement. The remaining gap is which features produce gains, for whom, and for how long. Comparisons holding feedback content and revision opportunities constant while separating explanation from recording would address the component question. L2’s gains also leave citation accuracy unresolved because its rubric did not score that outcome separately. [L2, p. 8, ¶1] Longer follow-up is also needed: L1 pooled no outcomes beyond six weeks, leaving the durability of later writing gains unresolved. [L1, p. 5, ¶1]
