# From Feedback to Independent Evidence-Based Argument Writing

Formative feedback can improve a draft without establishing that its writer can construct a stronger argument independently. L5 distinguishes correcting a particular text from learning to select evidence and judge what conclusions it supports when a comment is absent. A warrant is the reasoning connecting evidence to a conclusion, whether or not the writer states it explicitly [L5, p. 2, ¶1]. This distinction defines the central outcome for the present question: performance on a new argument-writing task, rather than uptake of comments or improvement in a revised essay. It also directs attention to whether feedback gives novices practice in judging evidence, beyond implementing particular corrections.

The aggregate evidence supports feedback for argument quality but leaves its benefits for independent writing less certain. Across 18 randomized or quasi-experimental comparisons involving 2,140 novice university students, L1 reported a pooled standardized mean difference of 0.31 (95% CI 0.16–0.46), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 61%). Quality generally encompassed claim clarity, relevant evidence and counterarguments [L1, p. 4, ¶1]. Fourteen comparisons assessed revisions, yielding 0.38 (95% CI 0.22–0.54); four assessed new tasks two to six weeks later, yielding 0.12 (95% CI −0.08–0.32). The latter interval permits both positive and negative effects. Without a reported interaction estimate, these results do not establish that feedback works differently across the two task categories [L1, p. 5, ¶1].

Identifying the conditions behind these results requires more than classifying comments by form. L1 distinguished directive comments proposing changes from questions asking writers to justify choices, but many interventions combined them, and descriptions were insufficient for a reliable comparison. Delivery, practice time and revision opportunities also varied [L1, p. 6, ¶2]. Eight comparisons had concerns about allocation or assessor masking, further limiting interpretation of the pooled evidence [L1, p. 5, ¶1]. L5 offers a process hypothesis: explaining revisions could provide practice in judging evidence, provided students understand the feedback and can act on it. Explanations could also merely repeat instructors’ wording, so their presence alone would not demonstrate that judgment [L5, p. 3, ¶2].

L2 provides experimental support for a package organized around explanation. In a required first-year course, 120 students were individually randomized to instructor comments alone or the same comments plus a worksheet asking which claim each cited finding supported and why a revision was justified. Evidence materials, writing time and revision opportunities were held constant. The worksheet group scored 0.6 points higher on revised essays (95% CI 0.2–1.0) and 0.5 points higher on an independently written essay using a new packet three weeks later (95% CI 0.1–0.9), both on a 0–10 scale. Worksheets were unavailable during the new essay, making this result relevant to independent performance [L2, p. 7, ¶1; p. 8, ¶1].

This finding supports the combined worksheet intervention, while leaving the contributions of its components unresolved: explanation prompts and a written revision record were not separated. Moreover, the follow-up analysis used 108 essays, with six students missing from each group; balanced counts do not resolve uncertainty about their unobserved performance [L2, p. 7, ¶1; p. 8, ¶1]. Interviews offer a possible account of the benefit: 11 of 16 volunteers said the worksheet helped them notice that a relevant citation might not justify a claim’s strength. These retrospective accounts do not establish changed understanding or mediation, and the supplied excerpt leaves recruitment and the remaining five accounts unclear [L2, p. 8, ¶2].

L3 shows why improvement during revision cannot itself establish independent learning. In its randomized trial of 90 novices, students using automated sentence-level suggestions gained 0.9 more points on their revised drafts than those receiving ordinary rubric guidance (95% CI 0.4–1.4). On a new essay two weeks later without the tool, the difference was 0.0 points (95% CI −0.4–0.4), which does not establish equivalence [L3, p. 3, ¶1; p. 4, ¶1]. This contrasts descriptively with L2, but cannot rank the interventions: although both used 0–10 scales, L3 emphasized coherence and evidence placement, whereas L2 included conflicting evidence [L2, p. 8, ¶1; L3, p. 4, ¶1]. Their comparators and follow-up timing also differed. Together, they support assessing later independent performance separately from revision gains.

L4 connects explanation with later writing outside a randomized intervention. Among 180 voluntary clinic attendees, students who explained feedback use scored 0.7 points higher on a subsequent 0–10 argument task (95% CI 0.2–1.2), adjusting for baseline writing and prior grades. Unmeasured motivation and instructor contact could account for this association [L4, p. 10, ¶1]. Lower-scoring students more often reported struggling to apply phrases such as “develop the warrant.” These accounts make intelligibility a candidate condition for using feedback, as L5 proposes, but do not demonstrate benefits from simpler wording [L4, p. 11, ¶2; L5, p. 3, ¶2]. L4 complements L2 without establishing a causal role for explanation; overlap between L1 and the individual studies is unspecified [L1, p. 6, ¶2].

The strongest supported answer is conditional: an explanation worksheet added to instructor comments improved independent argument quality in one course at a three-week follow-up, while the broader evidence does not identify a reliably superior feedback category [L1, p. 6, ¶2; L2, p. 8, ¶1]. Taken together with L5, these findings motivate testing feedback that students can interpret and use to justify evidence-to-claim connections; they do not establish those processes as necessary or sufficient conditions [L5, p. 3, ¶2]. The remaining gap is whether explanation itself, distinguished from revision records and other support, improves independent judgment across tasks and longer intervals; L1 pooled no outcomes beyond six weeks. Evidence attribution also requires direct assessment: L1 provided no specific pooled estimate, and L2 did not separately score citation accuracy [L1, p. 5, ¶1; L2, p. 8, ¶1]. Studies addressing these distinctions would clarify whether improved argument quality includes more accurate use of sources.
