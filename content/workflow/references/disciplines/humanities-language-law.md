# Humanities, language, history and law

Use for historical/archival inquiry, literary or discourse analysis, linguistics,
philosophical argument and doctrinal/comparative legal scholarship. These are
guidance lenses, not newly registered runtime subjects. Do not force all of them
into hypotheses, statistical tests or an IMRaD manuscript.

## Frame and collect sources — A/B

- State the interpretive, historical, conceptual, normative or empirical question.
  Define the corpus, period, language, jurisdiction or tradition and why that
  boundary permits the intended argument.
- Distinguish primary evidence, critical editions/translations, commentary and
  later scholarship. Keep author/creator, provenance, date, edition, locator and
  transmission/access limits; an archive's silence is not proof of nonexistence.
- For source searches, include alternate spellings, historical terminology,
  languages, catalog identifiers and citation chains when relevant. Record
  digitization and access bias rather than implying that searchable text is the
  complete historical record.

## Common approaches — C/E/I

| Work | Evidence and reasoning to retain | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| History / archival comparison | Provenance, authorship/purpose, chronology, source criticism, corroboration and conflicting accounts | Present-day categories projected backward; one surviving account treated as representative |
| Literary / discourse / close reading | Edition and passage locators, interpretive lens, linguistic/textual evidence, alternative readings | Plot summary substituted for analysis; quotations invented or detached from context |
| Corpus linguistics | Corpus boundary, genres, sampling, tokenization/annotation, units, language/edition and validation | Frequency treated as meaning; repeated authors/documents treated as independent evidence; OCR noise interpreted as change |
| Experimental linguistics | Participants and stimuli/items, counterbalancing, language proficiency, response measure and dependence | Ignoring item variability or inferring a general language property from one convenience sample |
| Philosophy / conceptual work | Definitions, premises, inference steps, objections, counterexamples and scope | Treating citation count as proof; presenting a contested premise as established fact |
| Doctrinal / comparative law | Jurisdiction, effective/as-of date, authority hierarchy, legal issue, holding/rule and interpretation | Treating commentary, dicta or another jurisdiction's rule as binding authority; assuming historical law is current |
| Socio-legal empirical work | Separate the legal proposition from behavioral evidence; document sampling/design and legal context | Claiming a law caused an outcome from timing or textual similarity alone |

## Common content to organize

Reuse source notes and the evidence ledger: claim ID → source/edition → page,
folio, paragraph, line or archive identifier → quotation/observation → inference
and alternative interpretation. Preserve original text beside a translation when
word choice drives the argument; identify translator and uncertain readings.
Check OCR/transcription against the image for consequential quotes or counts.

For legal research, keep a bounded authority table with jurisdiction, issuing
body/court, date, status, pinpoint and proposition supported. Verify current status
with official legislation/court sources when it affects the answer; if unavailable,
label the historical or unverified scope. Academic research does not authorize
case-specific legal advice, filing or contact with parties.

## Ethics and writing — D/F/G/H

Respect archive terms, image/quotation rights, oral-history consent and community
restrictions. Public availability does not remove every privacy or reuse condition.
Do not upload restricted archival or interview material to an external service
without authority for that use.

Build sections around the argument and source work. Show how interpretations
follow from evidence, address consequential alternative readings, and preserve
uncertainty about provenance or translation. For theory, distinguish a proved
result, a defended argument and a conjecture. Use venue-specific citation and
transliteration rules when verified; a biomedical checklist is not a default.

The [Library of Congress primary-source tools](https://www.loc.gov/programs/teachers/getting-started-with-primary-sources/guides/)
offer an observation/reflection/question framework for source analysis. They are
an introductory aid, not a sufficient standard for specialized historical or legal
claims; use the actual corpus, scholarly method and authoritative sources.
