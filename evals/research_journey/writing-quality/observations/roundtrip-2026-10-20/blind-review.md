## Q — Task A, English literature review

**Request scope: Pass.** The title, “From Revision to Independent Argument: Conditions for Learning from Formative Feedback,” identifies the requested subject. The concluding statement, “The remaining gap is which features produce gains, for whom, and for how long,” addresses the required research gap. The 900-word body uses all five sources and delivers a complete review. It connects L5's account of independent judgment [L5, p. 2, ¶1] with L2's new-task result [L2, p. 8, ¶1] and L1's follow-up limits [L1, p. 5, ¶1], matching `tasks.md`, Task A. Its sentence about possible component comparisons develops the research gap within the review rather than turning the answer into advice to the user.

**Argument continuity: Pass.** The opening distinction, “successful revision alone cannot establish independent learning,” controls the subsequent selection and interpretation of outcomes. After presenting L2 and L3, the sentence “L2 and L3 thus illustrate why revision and independent performance require separate assessment” explains the relevance of the comparison. The conclusion returns to the same outcome: “adding structured explanation and a revision record to usual comments improved independent writing in L2.” This is a connected progression from the construct [L5, p. 2, ¶1], through the paired revision/new-task findings [L2, p. 8, ¶1; L3, p. 4, ¶1], to a qualified answer. Although several paragraphs begin with individual sources, they contribute to one question rather than functioning as disconnected annotations.

**Explanatory depth: Pass.** The sentence “A warrant is the reasoning connecting evidence to a conclusion, whether or not it appears as an explicitly labelled sentence” makes the central concept accessible [L5, p. 2, ¶1]. The comparison explains why the two interventions cannot be ranked: “their comparators, follow-up periods and rubrics differed, with L2 assessing engagement with conflicting evidence and L3 emphasizing coherence and evidence placement” [L2, p. 7, ¶1; p. 8, ¶1; L3, p. 3, ¶1; p. 4, ¶1]. It also explains why the hypothesized mechanism remains unresolved: “the intervention combined explanation prompts with a written revision record, leaving their contributions unresolved” [L2, p. 8, ¶1]. These are evidential bridges from findings to interpretation, not cautions standing in for substantive analysis.

**Readable language: Pass.** “The later task prohibited worksheet access, supporting an effect extending beyond assisted revision” translates a design feature into its relevance to the reader [L2, p. 8, ¶1]. Likewise, “suggesting an accessibility problem without demonstrating an advantage of simpler wording” distinguishes a practical difficulty from tested efficacy in ordinary language [L4, p. 11, ¶2]. Quantitative passages are dense but intelligible for the specified research audience. Necessary terms are explained or used in context; references such as “the later task” have clear antecedents.

**Factual/citation fidelity: Pass.** “a pooled standardized mean difference of 0.31 (95% CI 0.16–0.46)” and “Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 61%)” accurately report the secondary synthesis rather than attributing its estimate to a single trial [L1, p. 4, ¶1]. For L2, “120 students were individually randomized” and “follow-up analysis included only 108 students, with six missing per group” preserve assignment and available-case follow-up [L2, p. 7, ¶1]. L3's “0.0 points (95% CI −0.4–0.4)” and L4's adjusted “0.7 points higher” are correctly attributed [L3, p. 4, ¶1; L4, p. 10, ¶1]. The concluding “its rubric did not score that outcome separately” correctly limits the citation-accuracy claim [L2, p. 8, ¶1]. Compound page/paragraph locators resolve to supplied passages; no invented source, numerical result or method was found.

**Inference fidelity: Pass.** “without a reported interaction estimate they do not establish that the subgroup effects differ statistically” preserves the limit on comparing L1's subgroups [L1, p. 5, ¶1]. “This interval does not establish equivalence” correctly limits L3's null-centered new-task result [L3, p. 4, ¶1]. The volunteer interviews “cannot establish changed understanding or mediation,” while “unmeasured motivation or instructor contact could account for the association” in L4 [L2, p. 8, ¶2; L4, p. 10, ¶1]. ## Within-task comparisons

### Task A: Q versus R

**Flow: slight preference for R.** R's transition, “Identifying the conditions behind these results requires more than classifying comments by form,” moves from the aggregate evidence directly to the conditions question. It presents L5's proposed process before assessing L2 and then links L4's intelligibility reports to that same process. This gives the final conditional synthesis a clear preparatory thread. Q is also coherent and has a local advantage in placing L2 and L3 next to each other before discussing the mechanism, which makes the revision/transfer comparison particularly easy to follow. The preference is modest and concerns organization, not correctness.

**Argument quality: slight preference for R.** R more explicitly integrates intelligibility, evidence-to-claim judgment, bundled intervention components and the distinction between argument quality and source accuracy into its answer about conditions. Its ending explains why the remaining measurement gap matters: “whether improved argument quality includes more accurate use of sources.” Q develops substantially the same bounded position and is more explicit about several procedural details, including L3's complete assessments and L4's unreported follow-up counts. Those details strengthen transparency but do not make Q's central argument more explanatory than R's. Both give substantive answers while acknowledging that the evidence cannot isolate necessary or sufficient conditions.

**Semantic fidelity: tie.** Both correctly preserve the L1 subgroup uncertainty, L2's positive unassisted result and bundled intervention, the non-mediating status of interviews, L3's uncertainty and different rubric, L4's confounding, and L5's conceptual status. Neither ranks the interventions causally or claims universal transfer. R's somewhat leaner reporting does not materially distort the evidence; Q's greater detail does not reveal a consequential error in R.

### Task B: P versus S

**Flow: slight preference for S.** S's discussion proceeds through three clearly separated functions: what the result adds relative to prior evidence, a possible explanation, and the limits on inference. P makes the same overall progression, but its dense first discussion paragraph carries the study interpretation, C2 comparison and C1 comparison together, while its final paragraph moves from mechanism to attrition and generalization. Both are readable and connected; S is a little easier to scan while preserving the argument.

**Argument quality: tie.** P offers the fuller explicit reconciliation with C1: an interval crossing zero in the synthesis does not make the own-study result contradictory, and differing interventions and scoring limit the comparison. P also retains C3's alternative possibility of added writing burden. S gives a particularly concrete comparison with C2 by explaining why the two 0.8-point findings do not repeat the same effect, and it explicitly distinguishes units across the own study's outcomes. These are different, defensible uses of the packet within the same short genre. Neither leaves a substantive gap that makes the other's overall argument decisively stronger.

**Semantic fidelity: tie.** Both preserve cluster assignment, delayed unassisted error identification, the clustering-adjusted estimate and interval, available-case analysis, equal-but-not-necessarily-unbiased absences, non-equivalence of practice outcomes, and the absence of measured mechanisms. Neither turns the result into general instrument proficiency or durable competence. P's baseline point concerns within-person change; S's concerns direct verification of initial comparability. Both are bounded and compatible with the study record.
