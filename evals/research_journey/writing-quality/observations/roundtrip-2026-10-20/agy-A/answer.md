**From Revision to Independent Argument: Conditions for Learning from Formative Feedback**

Formative feedback can improve a draft without necessarily strengthening a writer’s capacity to construct a later argument independently. L5 distinguishes correcting a text from learning to select relevant evidence and judge the conclusions it supports when a particular comment is absent. A warrant is the reasoning that connects evidence to a conclusion, whether or not it appears as an explicitly labelled sentence [L5, p. 2, ¶1]. This distinction makes performance on a new task central to evaluating feedback: successful revision alone cannot establish independent learning.

The strongest aggregate evidence concerns argument quality, typically assessed through claim clarity, evidence relevance, and treatment of counterarguments. Across 18 randomized or quasi-experimental comparisons involving 2,140 novice university students, L1 reported a pooled standardized mean difference of 0.31 (95% CI 0.16–0.46) favouring feedback over usual instruction or no additional feedback. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 61%), and eight comparisons raised concerns regarding allocation or assessor masking. Fourteen comparisons assessed revisions, yielding an estimate of 0.38 (95% CI 0.22–0.54), whereas only four assessed new tasks two to six weeks later, yielding 0.12 (95% CI −0.08–0.32). These findings provide clearer evidence for revision gains than for subsequent performance, but without a reported interaction estimate they do not establish a statistically significant difference between the subgroups. The confidence interval for new tasks also leaves both benefit and harm possible [L1, pp. 4–5, ¶1]. Because L1 does not specify whether the individual studies discussed below contributed to its synthesis, they cannot be treated as wholly independent confirmation [L1, p. 6, ¶2].

L2 provides more specific evidence that a structured revision activity can support subsequent writing. In a first-year course, 120 students were individually randomized to receive either usual instructor comments or those same comments paired with a worksheet asking them to identify which claim each cited finding supported and to justify their revisions. Evidence materials, writing time, and the single revision opportunity were held constant, and assessors were masked. On a 0–10 argument-quality scale, the worksheet group scored 0.6 points higher on the revised essay (95% CI 0.2–1.0) and 0.5 points higher three weeks later on an independent essay using a new evidence packet (95% CI 0.1–0.9). The later task prohibited access to the worksheet, supporting an effect that extended beyond assisted revision. However, the follow-up analysis included only 108 students, with six missing per group; equal attrition across groups does not establish that missingness was inconsequential [L2, pp. 7–8, ¶1].

Such transfer to unassisted writing does not occur across all feedback interventions. L3 randomized 90 novices to receive either immediate automated suggestions on clarity and evidence placement or ordinary written rubric guidance, with one 45-minute revision session per group. All students completed both assessments, which were scored on a 0–10 scale by masked assessors. The automated-tool group gained 0.9 points more on the revised draft (95% CI 0.4–1.4), but two weeks later, the between-group difference on a new essay written without the tool was 0.0 points (95% CI −0.4–0.4). This interval does not establish equivalence [L3, pp. 3–4, ¶1]. Together, L2 and L3 illustrate why revision and independent performance require separate assessment. Even so, they do not establish that explanation worksheets outperform automated feedback: their comparators, follow-up periods, and rubrics differed, with L2 assessing engagement with conflicting evidence and L3 emphasizing coherence and evidence placement [L2, p. 8, ¶1; L3, p. 4, ¶1].

The worksheet findings align with L5’s proposal that explaining revisions may offer practice in judging evidential support, although consistency alone does not demonstrate that mechanism. L5 also cautions that student explanations may simply repeat an instructor’s wording [L5, p. 3, ¶2]. In L2, 11 of 16 interviewed worksheet users reported that the worksheet helped them notice when a relevant citation did not justify the strength of a claim. Because these retrospective accounts came from volunteers and the study described neither the remaining five accounts nor the recruitment procedures, they cannot establish changed understanding or mediation. Moreover, the intervention combined explanation prompts with a written revision record, leaving the separate contribution of each component unresolved [L2, p. 8, ¶¶1–2].

Observational evidence likewise supports examining explanation while underscoring the difficulty of isolating its effect. Among 180 first-year writing-clinic volunteers in L4, students who explained their use of feedback in a revision memo scored 0.7 points higher on a subsequent 0–10 argument task (95% CI 0.2–1.2) after adjustment for baseline writing and prior course grade. Because explanation was not randomized, unmeasured motivation or instructor contact could account for the association, and follow-up missingness was not reported [L4, p. 10, ¶1]. While this finding complements L2 by identifying a similar pattern in a voluntary clinic setting, it does not independently establish that explanation causes improvement.

L5’s proposal also depends on students being able to interpret feedback and having an opportunity to act on it [L5, p. 3, ¶2]. In L4, students with lower baseline scores more frequently described phrases such as “develop the warrant” as difficult to apply, suggesting an accessibility barrier without demonstrating that simpler wording improves outcomes [L4, p. 11, ¶2]. Available evidence also cannot identify a preferred comment format. L1 distinguished directive comments proposing specific changes from questions prompting writers to justify their choices, but found that interventions frequently combined both formats and that too few adequately described comparisons existed to estimate a reliable difference. Delivery methods, practice time, and revision opportunities also varied across studies [L1, p. 6, ¶2].

Taken together, these sources support a conditional conclusion: adding structured explanation and a revision record to usual instructor comments improved independent writing in L2, but the evidence does not establish that explanation alone is necessary or sufficient for improvement. Key open questions concern which intervention features produce gains, for which learners, and over what duration. Trials that hold feedback content and revision opportunities constant while separating explanation prompts from revision recording would help isolate the active components. L2’s gains also leave citation accuracy unresolved because its rubric did not score that outcome separately [L2, p. 8, ¶1]. Finally, longer follow-up is needed: L1 pooled no outcomes beyond six weeks, leaving the durability of subsequent writing gains unknown [L1, p. 5, ¶1].
